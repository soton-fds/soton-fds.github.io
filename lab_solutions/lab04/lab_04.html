read_csv2 is designed for the convention used in much of Europe, where ; is the delimiter and , is the decimal mark (e.g. 14,99). It therefore treats the . in 14.99 as a thousands separator, so the price 14.99 becomes 1499 and the rating 4.2 becomes 42. Notice that R did not give an error; the only warning was an info message that is easy to ignore. This is why you should always check your loaded data against the raw file!

Any calculation involving an NA gives NA, since R can’t know what the missing value is. Setting na.rm = TRUE removes the missing values before computing the mean.

students |>
    filter(name != "Henry Zhao") |>
    summarise(mean_score1 = mean(score1, na.rm = TRUE), mean_average = mean(average))
# A tibble: 1 × 2
  mean_score1 mean_average
        <dbl>        <dbl>
1        80.2         79.3

Henry’s notes say he was “Absent”, so his scores of 0 really mean “did not take the test”, and should arguably be missing (NA) rather than 0. Including him lowers the mean of score1 from about 80 to about 71. Whether that is right depends on the question: for “how well did students who sat the test do?” he should be excluded, but for “what marks were awarded?” a 0 might be correct. This kind of problem can’t be detected by R; you have to look at the data.

It contains information about 53940 diamonds. Each row is a diamond, and each column contains information about that diamond such as:

  • price: The price of the diamond in US dollars.
  • carat: The weight of the diamond (1 carat = 0.2g).
  • cut: Quality of the cut.
  • color: The color of the diamond.
  • clarity: A measure of how clear the diamond is.
  • depth: Total depth percentage; \(= \frac{z}{\frac{1}{2}(x + y)} \times 100\).
  • table: Width of the top of the diamond relative to the widest point.
  • x: Length of the diamond in mm.
  • y: Width of the diamond in mm.
  • z: Depth of the diamond in mm.

Ideal diamonds are, on average, the smallest, and size has a much bigger effect on price than cut does. So Ideal diamonds are cheaper on average not because of their cut, but because they tend to be smaller. When two variables (here cut and carat) are related like this, comparing averages for one of them can be very misleading; this is called confounding. The price-per-carat in the next question partly adjusts for size.

The table is not tidy. Each observation is one student’s score on one assessment, and the variables are the student, the semester, the assessment and the score.

  • Each variable is a column: the semester and the assessment are variables, but their values (e.g. Semester1, A1) are stored in the column names rather than in columns of their own.

Both approaches give the same result here. However, -student_id means “every column except student_id”, so a new student_name column would be pivoted too, mixing names in with the scores (in fact pivot_longer would give an error, since it can’t combine text and numbers in one column). starts_with("Semester") only picks out the assessment columns, so it would keep working. Choosing columns by what they are, rather than by what they are not, is usually more robust.

One of the key principles of tidy data is that each cell should contain only one value. Notice that in the new dataset, the column assessment contains two values: the Semester and the Assessment ID, separated by an _.
