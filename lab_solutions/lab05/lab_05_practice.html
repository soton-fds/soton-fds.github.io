Joining Data

These questions follow on from Tasks 1 and 1b of the lab. (Make sure your outputs print character names, not just IDs).

Solution. Which characters have lines in the most episodes?

We count the episodes for each character_id first, and only then join to simpsons_characters to get the names. This way the join is done on a small summary table (one row per character) rather than on all 158,255 script lines.

simpsons_script_lines |>
    group_by(character_id) |>
    summarise(num_episodes = n_distinct(episode_id), .groups = "drop") |>
    arrange(desc(num_episodes)) |>
    inner_join(simpsons_characters, by = c("character_id" = "id")) |>
    select(character_name, num_episodes) |>
    head(10)
# A tibble: 10 × 2
   character_name      num_episodes
   <chr>                      <int>
 1 Homer Simpson                564
 2 Marge Simpson                563
 3 Bart Simpson                 562
 4 Lisa Simpson                 562
 5 Moe Szyslak                  376
 6 Milhouse Van Houten          325
 7 Lenny Leonard                319
 8 Chief Wiggum                 318
 9 Seymour Skinner              297
10 Carl Carlson                 282

Notice that grouping by character_id also creates a group for NA, the scene directions with no character (see Task 1 of the lab). This group appears in 564 episodes, tying with Homer for first place! The inner_join quietly removes it, because NA has no match in simpsons_characters; with a left_join it would appear near the top of the table with no name. It would be clearer still to remove it on purpose, with filter(!is.na(character_id)) before grouping.

Which characters appear together most frequently in episodes?

First we make a table with one row for each character in each episode, dropping the scene directions (which have no character):

episode_characters <- simpsons_script_lines |>
    filter(!is.na(character_id)) |>
    select(episode_id, character_id) |>
    distinct()

To find pairs of characters in the same episode, we join this table to itself on episode_id. Each episode has many characters on both sides of the join, so this is a many-to-many join: every character in an episode is matched with every other character in that episode. As the notes say, dplyr warns about many-to-many joins because they are often a mistake, but here it is exactly what we want, so we say so with relationship = "many-to-many", which also silences the warning.

episode_characters |>
    inner_join(episode_characters, by = "episode_id", relationship = "many-to-many") |>
    # both tables have a character_id column, so they get .x and .y suffixes.
    # Keeping only character_id.x < character_id.y removes each character paired
    # with themselves, and counts each pair once rather than twice (as A-B and B-A).
    filter(character_id.x < character_id.y) |>
    group_by(character_id.x, character_id.y) |>
    summarise(episodes_together = n(), .groups = "drop") |>
    # join back to characters twice, to get both names (again with .x and .y suffixes)
    left_join(simpsons_characters, by = c("character_id.x" = "id")) |>
    left_join(simpsons_characters, by = c("character_id.y" = "id")) |>
    select(character_name.x, character_name.y, episodes_together) |>
    arrange(desc(episodes_together)) |>
    head(20)

Extension: using a regular expression

In a regular expression, | means “or”, so "donut|doughnut" matches either word. As in the lab, we convert the text to lower case first, so that “Donut” and “DONUTS” are matched too.

simpsons_script_lines |>
    filter(str_detect(str_to_lower(spoken_words), "donut|doughnut")) |>
    distinct(episode_id) |>
    nrow()
[1] 56

A more compact pattern is "do(ugh)?nut": the brackets group ugh together, and ? means “the previous thing is optional”, so this matches “donut” and “doughnut”.

simpsons_script_lines |>
    filter(str_detect(str_to_lower(spoken_words), "do(ugh)?nut")) |>
    distinct(episode_id) |>
    nrow()
[1] 56

Tidying the capitalisation of location names

A name is written in capitals exactly when converting it to upper case leaves it unchanged:

simpsons_locations |>
    filter(location_name == str_to_upper(location_name)) |>
    nrow()
[1] 2594

That’s more than half of the 4,459 locations. We can use str_to_title to put every name in “title case”, and look at a few examples:

simpsons_locations |>
    mutate(title_name = str_to_title(location_name)) |>
    filter(location_name %in% c("KITCHEN", "Simpson Home", "TV Studio", "Circus of Values", "NEIGHBOR #1'S HOUSE")) |>
    select(location_name, title_name)
# A tibble: 5 × 2
  location_name       title_name         
  <chr>               <chr>              
1 Simpson Home        Simpson Home       
2 KITCHEN             Kitchen            
3 Circus of Values    Circus Of Values   
4 NEIGHBOR #1'S HOUSE Neighbor #1'S House
5 TV Studio           Tv Studio

str_to_title simply capitalises the first letter of each word and puts the rest in lower case. That works well for KITCHEN, but it doesn’t know that “TV” is an abbreviation or that short words like “of” are usually left in lower case, and it is confused by the apostrophe in #1'S. Names that were already sensibly capitalised can be made worse. One improvement is to convert only the names that are in capitals, using if_else (see the Week 2 notes):

simpsons_locations |>
    mutate(location_name = if_else(
        location_name == str_to_upper(location_name),
        str_to_title(location_name),
        location_name
    )) |>
    filter(id %in% c(6, 14)) |>
    select(id, location_name)
# A tibble: 2 × 2
     id location_name   
  <dbl> <chr>           
1     6 Kitchen         
2    14 Circus of Values

Even so, for names that were entirely in capitals, information such as which words were abbreviations has been lost, and no function can recover it perfectly. Automatic cleaning like this is usually good enough for display, but it’s worth checking the results rather than assuming they are right.

Dates

These questions follow on from Tasks 3a and 3b of the lab.

In simpsons_episodes, read_csv has already recognised original_air_date as a date.

Solution. Which day of the week did episodes first air?

simpsons_episodes |>
    mutate(weekday = format(original_air_date, "%A")) |>
    count(weekday, sort = TRUE)
# A tibble: 5 × 2
  weekday       n
  <chr>     <int>
1 Sunday      506
2 Thursday     89
3 Tuesday       2
4 Wednesday     2
5 Friday        1

Most episodes aired on a Sunday, but 89 aired on a Thursday. Counting the Thursday episodes by season shows that they are all from seasons 2 to 5:

simpsons_episodes |>
    mutate(weekday = format(original_air_date, "%A")) |>
    filter(weekday == "Thursday") |>
    count(season)
# A tibble: 4 × 2
  season     n
   <dbl> <int>
1      2    22
2      3    24
3      4    21
4      5    22

In fact, The Simpsons moved to Thursday nights between 1990 and 1994, before moving back to Sundays.

The next question uses the stock market data from Task 3b of the lab.

Solution. We group by both the month number and the month name. The name makes the output readable, but on its own it would sort the months alphabetically (April, August, December, …); including the number keeps them in calendar order.

stock_data |>
    mutate(month = month(date), month_name = format(date, "%B")) |>
    group_by(month, month_name) |>
    summarise(mean_volume = mean(volume), .groups = "drop")
