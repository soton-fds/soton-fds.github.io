Data Ethics and Biases

Objectives

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • identify ethical and regulatory considerations in a data science project
  • recognise different types of bias, and their impact
  • evaluate real-world cases, and document data and models with data cards and model cards

No code today: this is a discussion lecture. Every “Task” works well as a 1–2 minute talk-to-your-neighbour, then take two or three answers before revealing.

A case study

Amazon’s recruiting tool

  • Amazon built a tool to score job applicants’ CVs automatically
  • Training data: every CV submitted to the company over the previous ten years, and who was hired
  • By 2015 they found it was not gender neutral: given equally skilled applicants, it preferred men
ImportantTask

Why? The tool was never told the applicants’ gender.

It didn’t learn what makes a good candidate — it learned what Amazon’s past hires looked like, and past hiring in tech had favoured men (and most applicants were men).

Source: the Reuters report, linked from the notes. Let them discuss before revealing — someone usually gets there.

Removing gender isn’t enough

Amazon edited the tool to ignore the terms found to penalise women. It still couldn’t be trusted:

  • “captain of the women’s chess club”
  • all-women colleges
  • men and women describing their experience in different words

These are proxies: variables that stand in for an attribute that was removed. A postcode can be a proxy for ethnicity or income.

Amazon scrapped the tool.

It’s not just Amazon

  • COMPAS, used in some US courts to predict reoffending: a 2016 ProPublica investigation found Black defendants who did not reoffend were labelled high risk nearly twice as often as white defendants
  • Credit scoring: lower scores for freelancers and gig workers, whose income is documented differently — regardless of whether they are financially stable

We can’t prevent every bias. Our job: recognise it, mitigate it, and alert the people who rely on our work.

Types of bias

Six kinds of bias

  • Sampling: the sample doesn’t represent the population
  • Selection: whether a record appears depends on the outcome being studied
  • Historical: the data are accurate, but the past was unfair
  • Measurement: data are collected or measured inaccurately
  • Confirmation: we see what we expected to see
  • Algorithmic: a model’s results are systematically prejudiced, because of its data or design

Not an exhaustive list. Historical bias is the subtle one: perfect sampling doesn’t fix it, because the data faithfully record an unfair past.

Name that bias

ImportantTask

Which kind of bias is each?

  1. A survey on smartphone use is run only among university students.
  2. A study of successful business strategies looks only at companies still trading today.
  3. A fitness tracker undercounts steps for people who walk slowly or with an irregular gait.
  4. An analyst who believes remote work boosts productivity focuses on the survey answers that agree.
  5. A face recognition system trained mostly on lighter-skinned faces misidentifies darker-skinned people more often.
  6. The Amazon tool.
  1. Sampling — young people over-represented
  2. Selection — the failed companies are missing (survivorship)
  3. Measurement
  4. Confirmation
  5. Algorithmic — caused by sampling bias in its training data
  6. Historical mainly (it imitated past decisions), plus sampling (mostly male applicants) → algorithmic

Patterns are the point

Every model relies on finding patterns — that’s what it’s for.

  • legitimate: “applicants with higher marks in data science tend to do better in the job”
  • not: patterns that disadvantage people because of who they are — e.g. their sex or race

Removing protected characteristics doesn’t stop it (proxies). The way to know is to check: compare results across groups.

Ethics by design

Ask the questions at every stage, not at the end:

  • Collection: do we need all this data? (data minimisation)
  • Analysis: who is represented, who is missing? Could the results be biased against a group?
  • Sharing: could results identify individuals, or be used unfairly? Are they presented honestly? (week 6’s honest plots)
  • Documentation: are these decisions written down? (data cards, model cards)

Reproducibility (week 3) is part of this: it lets others check exactly how a result was produced.

The law

A disclaimer

Caution

We focus on the UK. This is a complex area and the law changes: in real work, check the current rules and ask an expert — especially when handling personal data, protected characteristics, or data that could affect people’s rights.

Personal data

Any information relating to an identified or identifiable living person.

  • Anonymised: individuals can no longer be identified → no longer personal data
  • Pseudonymised: names replaced by IDs, with the link kept separately → still personal data
ImportantTask

A dataset has had names removed, but keeps postcode, date of birth and sex. Is it anonymous?

Almost certainly not. A well-known study found that 87% of the US population can be uniquely identified by ZIP code, date of birth and sex alone (Sweeney, 2000). Joining to another dataset (week 5) can re-identify people.

UK GDPR

Personal data must be:

  • used fairly, lawfully and transparently
  • used for specified, explicit purposes
  • adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary
  • accurate, and kept up to date
  • kept no longer than necessary
  • handled securely

Data minimisation in practice: read only the columns you need (col_select, week 10), drop identifying columns early, share summaries rather than records (week 7’s databases).

Fines: up to £17.5 million or 4% of annual global turnover, whichever is higher — plus reputational damage, compensation claims and disruption from investigations.

Two lists

Special category data (UK GDPR)

How sensitive data must be handled:

  • race, ethnic background
  • political opinions
  • religious beliefs
  • trade union membership
  • genetics, biometrics
  • health
  • sex life, sexual orientation

Protected characteristics (Equality Act 2010)

How people must not be treated:

  • age
  • disability
  • gender reassignment
  • marriage, civil partnership
  • pregnancy, maternity
  • race
  • religion or belief
  • sex
  • sexual orientation

The Amazon tool used no special category data — but discriminating by sex may still be unlawful.

Documenting data and models

Data cards

A data card does for a dataset what documentation does for a function: what it is, and how it may — and may not — be used.

Our minimal version:

  • name, version/date, short description, source
  • intended use (and forbidden uses)
  • composition: rows, columns, key variables
  • protected attributes present?
  • missingness
  • collection method
  • known limitations / biases
  • privacy / sensitivity
  • licence, contact

The full framework (Pushkarna et al., 2022; the Data Cards Playbook) is the “gold standard” and very long. Kaggle has users fill in partial data cards. In the assessment students may be asked to complete parts of a data card.

Write part of a data card

ImportantTask

The Kaggle Titanic dataset: 891 passengers, with Survived, Pclass, Name, Sex, Age, Fare, Cabin, … Age is missing for 177 rows, Cabin for 687. Fill in:

  • protected attributes present?
  • known limitations / biases
  • privacy / sensitivity
  • Protected attributes: yes — sex and age
  • Limitations / biases: not representative of modern populations; survival depends on class and sex because of historical boarding policies (“women and children first”) — not causal for modern uses; ~20% of ages and ~77% of cabins missing
  • Privacy: contains names, but the passengers have all died, so UK GDPR (living people only) doesn’t apply; public historical data, low risk

Model cards

The equivalent for a model: intended use, performance, fairness assessment, limitations, risks.

ImportantTask

You’re asked to feed applicant data into this model. What concerns you, and what would you do?

  • Model: Loan Approval Classifier v1.0
  • Intended use: assisting bank staff; NOT fully automated decisions
  • Performance: accuracy 85%, precision 80%, recall 78%
  • Protected attributes: gender, ethnicity, age — used for fairness analysis, not as predictors
  • Fairness: no significant gender bias; slight bias for age 60+
  • Limitations: may not generalise outside the training region; sensitive to missing income data
  • the age 60+ bias: age is a protected characteristic — test it on our own applicants, and flag it
  • human review is required: attach that warning to our outputs; don’t let it become an automatic decision
  • extrapolation: check our applicants come from the training region, and how much income data is missing
  • proxies: “not used as predictors” doesn’t rule out indirect bias

Real examples: model cards on Hugging Face (e.g. BERT’s). Mitchell et al. (2019) introduced them. Students won’t build ML models in this course, but they need to read model cards as consumers.

Wrap-up

Our job as data scientists

  • look for bias at every stage — and don’t assume your data are representative or your model is fair
  • mitigate what you can, and tell stakeholders about the rest
  • know when personal data, special category data or protected characteristics are involved — and get expert advice
  • document data and models with data cards and model cards
